Coal mine methane in Kazakhstan

Peer-reviewed article, 2024. An economic and environmental analysis of a project that captures methane at coal mines in the Karaganda region and turns it into electricity.

Central Asian Journal of Sustainability and Climate Research, 2024, vol. 3, no. 1, pp. 96–125. Published 31 July 2024.

Coal mine methane in Kazakhstan: economic and environmental case study

We are convinced that capturing and using coal mine methane must become an urgent investment and policy priority in Kazakhstan — to meet the 2030 methane pledge, prevent further mine disasters and deliver strong economic returns.

Sergey Vassilyev, Galym IskakovEnvironmental Fund for the Development of Almaty

01 Context

The price of methane is miners' lives

From 2004 to 2023, 161 people died in explosions at ArcelorMittal Temirtau mines in the Karaganda region. The worst recent accident was at the Kostenko mine in October 2023, with 46 dead. After it, the company changed hands and was renamed Qarmet.

Methane is also a powerful greenhouse gas: over 20 years it warms the atmosphere 84–87 times more than CO₂. At COP28 Kazakhstan pledged to cut methane emissions by 30% by 2030 and to reach carbon neutrality by 2060. The country's coal-bed methane reserves are estimated at 2.0–4.3 trillion m³, and almost none of it is used.

02 Project

40 megawatts from mine gas

In 2013 the US Environmental Protection Agency prepared a pre-feasibility study: capture methane at six mines and burn it in small gas-engine power plants, 2–3 per mine, linked by one pipeline. The project was never built.

  • 40 MW capacity in two phases: 19 and 21 MW
  • About 298 million kWh a year over a 30-year lifetime
  • Uses more than 226 million m³ of methane a year
  • Cost: $54 million in 2013 prices, $72 million in 2024 prices
03 Method

How we did the analysis

A cost-benefit analysis using discounted cash flows. We collected public data and the views of energy and finance experts, and visited the pilot methane power plant at the Lenin mine and a large plant in Doncaster, UK.

Data collection

Technical, environmental and economic data and local context.

Valuation

Private and public benefits, capital and operating costs. Market and shadow prices, the value of a statistical life.

Discounting

A private rate (WACC 9.5%) and a social rate of time preference (9.2%).

Results

Net present value, scenarios and sensitivity analysis.

Power plant investorRevenue from selling electricity and carbon credits.
Mine ownerHigher productivity, lower accident compensation and environmental payments.
SocietyLives saved, better health and harvests, fewer greenhouse gases.
04 Benefits

What one life saved is worth

To value lives saved, we estimated the value of a statistical life for Qarmet workers at $1.6–4.8 million. The model uses the lower bound. Compensation to the family of one miner who dies costs the company at least $300,000.

Every tonne of methane kept out of the air saves society about $100: methane forms ground-level ozone, which damages crops and health. Kazakhstan's methane emission charge, by contrast, is symbolic — the project would save only about $10,000 a year on it.

05 Results

Good for society; for business only with carbon credits

For the country the project is clearly worthwhile: an economic NPV of $243 million and a 42% return. Even with a zero carbon price and no benefit from lower ozone, the public value stays positive, from $48 million.

For business, without selling carbon credits, the project loses money: −$27.7 million. With credits it reaches +$15.6 million and a 12% return, but only if the mine owner takes part in the project itself. For an outside investor the gain is close to zero.

06 Sensitivity

The tariff matters more than construction cost

We tested how the result for Qarmet without credits changes with costs and tariffs. A 10% rise in costs cuts the project's value by 25%. A 17% rise in the tariff — from ¢3.0 to ¢3.5 per kWh — improves it by 51%.

The project turns positive at a tariff above ¢4.3 per kWh — the average price at the 2023 renewable energy auctions — with costs of $54–79 million. Outlined cells mark the break-even line.

07 Financing

Green bonds close the gaps

We modelled four scenarios with Qarmet as the only investor. Seven-year loans at 7.3% create dips in cash flow. Green bonds repaid at maturity remove this burden — scenarios 3 and 4 stay positive from the early years.

08 Recommendations

What it takes to make the project happen

The mine owner takes part

Qarmet should be both investor and developer — or pass all project benefits to outside investors.

Working markets

A local carbon market, or admitting mine-methane power plants to renewable auctions as biogas plants already are.

Stable tariffs

A guaranteed price for electricity from methane and other public incentives.

Green bonds

The best form of debt finance: no regular repayments and no cash gaps.

Why mining leaders are in no hurry to switch to renewables

Qazaq Green magazine, 2024. What keeps Kazakhstan's four largest mining groups from switching to renewable energy.

Overcoming obstacles: why are the leaders of the mining industry in no hurry to switch to renewable energy?

In our view, Kazakhstan's mining giants must speed up their move to renewable energy — but this will not happen without targeted policy and financial incentives that address the sector's real constraints.

01 Context

Renewables grow, mining stays aside

Renewable energy in Kazakhstan has boomed over the past decade, but mining companies have built almost no plants of their own. There are only a few: in 2019 a Kazakhmys subsidiary launched the 10 MW Kengir solar plant in the Ulytau region, in 2023 the 50 MW Balkhash plant, which was due to be expanded to 100 MW by the end of 2025.

The rest is still on paper: the 155 MW Khromtau-1 wind farm at ERG's Donskoy plant and cascades of small hydro plants of up to 106 MW involving KAZ Minerals. The article looks at the four largest groups: Kazakhmys / KAZ Minerals, KazZinc, ERG and Qarmet.

02 First barrier

Their own coal power is cheaper

Although renewables keep getting cheaper, they still lose to the coal and hydro plants the mining giants own. The average tariff of these plants is ₸7.55 per kWh. The cheapest solar power at the 2023 auctions cost ₸13.89, and wind ₸10.38.

On top of that, the “single group of persons” rule lets companies buy electricity from their own plants directly, bypassing the Single Buyer. Of all renewables, only wind can more or less compete on price.

03 Second barrier

A mine cannot wait for the wind

Much of the mining equipment must run without stopping. A sudden outage does more than cut output: ventilation failures let dangerous gases build up, and stopped hoists can trap miners underground.

Sun and wind are intermittent. Energy storage solves this but makes a plant much more expensive — and then even wind farms lose to coal and hydro.

04 Third barrier

Debt eats up investment

The heavy financial obligations of almost every operating mining company leave no money for projects outside the core business. Banks prefer to finance what companies already know how to do: expand existing plants rather than build renewables from scratch.

Besides, among miners' environmental priorities decarbonisation is not first — nature protection and workplace safety matter more. The closure of the Cobre Panama copper mine and ArcelorMittal's exit from Kazakhstan showed the cost of neglecting waste and worker safety.

05 What to do

A comprehensive approach is needed

The move to renewables is a global trend, and in the long run Kazakhstan's companies will have to switch to clean energy. The groundwork must be laid now, with government, financial institutions and industry acting together for a managed, secure and socially responsible transition.

New financing mechanisms

Instruments that allow investment in renewables without new debt.

Tax incentives

Exemption from some taxes in exchange for investment in renewable energy.

Joint research

Work on energy storage and grid stability.

How to bring nature back to Karasu

Karasu Lake Conservation Plan, draft of January 2025. Three levels of action: from protecting the green shores from construction to nest boxes, floating islands and camera traps.

Draft, January 2025. 32 pages. Prepared by our partner organisation.

Karasu Lake Conservation Plan

We believe this plan should be adopted and urgently replicated for every water body in the Almaty region, as a model for reconciling urban growth with biodiversity. EFRA calls on policymakers and donors to endorse it and to fund similar master plans across the region.

Khatia Basilashvili, Giorgi LemonjavaOrganization for “Environmental Research and Species Conservation”, Georgia

Download the full plan

  • 4 haof continuous green space not cut up by buildings and roads

01 The lake today

The green zone has halved

Karasu is an artificial reservoir created by a dam on the river. Its level is held by pressurised groundwater, so it barely depends on rainfall. In 2023–2024 the lake was drained to remove silt and litter; how this affected the ecosystem has yet to be assessed.

Satellite images show that the green and blue space around the lake shrank from about 13 to 7 hectares between 2002 and 2024. The main cause is construction. The narrower the green buffer, the worse it filters water, so the first task is to stop the loss of greenery and fix its boundaries clearly, including the marshes upstream.

There is no baseline data on the lake's biodiversity, so the authors estimate the likely fauna from similar places around Almaty.

02 Approach

Three levels of action

Goals must be realistic and take residents' needs into account. Levels 2 and 3 do not depend on the first: they can start even before the green zone's boundaries are fixed.

Protect the greenery

Keep the unbuilt land around the lake and along the river and fix its boundaries.

Improve habitats

A semi-natural park, quiet zones by the water, a living shoreline of native plants.

Help specific species

Nest boxes, insect hotels, shelters for reptiles and wildlife monitoring.

03 Park

A semi-natural park instead of a Soviet-style square

People live all around the lake, so conservation must not take away space for recreation. The answer is a semi-natural park: less concrete and fewer decorative exotic plants, more native species and natural relief, with paths for walking and cycling.

  • Small quiet refuges right by the water, with no path between the thickets and the lake
  • A living shoreline instead of steep concrete: reeds, fallen trees, rock piles
  • A large protected zone in the south of the lake, where soil is now being dumped — with wooden viewing piers only
  • An example: the restored waterway in the UC Davis Arboretum, California
04 Plants

Native species, not exotics

More than 70% of Almaty's urban greenery is introduced species, and even the plant lists the city recommends are mostly non-native. Karasu lies in a zone of high endemism: Kazakhstan has 5,658 species of vascular plants, many found nowhere else.

As a benchmark, the authors suggest the natural riverside communities of the Ili River, 90 km north of Almaty. The exact planting list should be drawn up after a field survey. Native plants already growing at the lake — such as water lilies and marsh vegetation — should be kept, and invasive species removed.

05 Birds

Putting Karasu on the birdwatchers' map

Birds are a good indicator of ecosystem health. But there is no data on Karasu's birds: the lake is not in the eBird database. It can become a hotspot if residents record sightings and birdwatching events are held. At the similar Malaya Almatinka lake, 7 km away, 51 species have been recorded — from mallards and grey herons to kingfishers.

The first practical step is nest boxes for cavity-nesting birds. In California, the Putah Creek Nestbox Highway has put up 200 boxes since 2000, and over 21 years more than 13,000 chicks of nine species have fledged from them. Each species needs its own box and entrance size so that predators cannot get in.

06 Water, islands and pollinators

Simple measures to build with residents

Floating islands

Made of pipes, mesh, organic mats and native plants such as reeds. The roots clean the water, fish spawn beneath, waterbirds nest on top. Built by Ramsar Heroes in 2025.

Insect hotels

Bundles of hollow stems or drilled blocks with 0.4–1.2 cm tunnels for solitary bees and wasps. Natural materials only, a rain cover, entrances facing south-east.

Help in winter

Rock piles and undisturbed earth mounds where reptiles, amphibians and hedgehogs can overwinter, feeders for tits. No needless interference: a healthy habitat matters more than feeding.

07 Mammals and reptiles

First, find out who lives here

There is no data on mammals at Karasu, so the first step is a baseline survey with camera traps. The plan includes a step-by-step protocol that students and volunteers can follow: preparing and labelling cameras, capture settings, mounting the sensor 30–40 cm high, facing north or south and away from the water to avoid false triggers.

Of the 15 reptile species known in and around Almaty, 8 have been seen in the last ten years. By a lake with a peat marsh, grass snakes, the Dione rat snake and the sand lizard are the most likely finds. Rock piles, logs and burrows will help them. There is no data on amphibians at all — studying them is also part of the plan.

08 Risks

What could get in the way

01Theft of camera traps

Place cameras out of sight, with locks, and explain to residents why they are there.

02Cats and dogs

They hunt birds, reptiles and small mammals. Keep pets under control, especially in spring; in future, no-walking zones.

03Rats and squirrels

They can push out other species. Camera traps will show the scale of the problem; then trapping or relocation.

04Water health

Beneficial microorganisms should be added only with professional hydrologists, or they may do harm.

05No aquatic life

Plant native aquatic plants — cattails and reeds — and bring back native fish, amphibians and invertebrates.

06Invasive species

Regular monitoring to spot them early and remove them quickly.

Money for nature, forever

An assessment of whether Kazakhstan can launch a Project Finance for Permanence — a deal that locks in money for protected areas for 15 years ahead. Prepared by EFRA for the UNEP-WCMC SPACES-Kazakhstan project, 2025.

Viability assessment for a Project Finance for Permanence (PFP) initiative in Kazakhstan

In our view, a PFP can become a transformative, nationally anchored financing mechanism for Kazakhstan's biodiversity goals — and the Government and donors should now move from viability assessment to design and negotiation.

01 The idea

One deal instead of a dozen short projects

Conservation is usually funded by donor projects lasting 3–5 years: the money runs out and the work stops. A PFP brings government, donors and business into a single deal: everyone agrees up front on money for many years and on legally binding conservation commitments. Funds are released as conditions are met.

Such deals already work in Brazil, Colombia, Peru, Bhutan, Costa Rica, Canada and Mongolia. Kazakhstan is noticeably richer per head than most of these countries, so more money can come from within the country, and a PFP becomes more of a common framework for different sources than a single financial instrument.

02 Where

From 11% of the country towards 30×30

Kazakhstan's protected areas now cover 31 million ha, 11.4% of the country. The Biodiversity Conservation Concept for 2025–2035 expands them to 34 million ha (12.5%), and with additional proposed sites to 41 million ha (15%).

To get closer to the global 30×30 target, other effective area-based conservation measures (OECMs) are also needed: areas that protect nature without being reserves. The most valuable are Ramsar sites, Important Bird Areas and river deltas; the largest by area are hunting grounds and pastures.

03 Money

Twelve mechanisms, half of them fit a PFP

The SPACES programme built a financial and economic model for the government and identified 12 instruments that could raise $342 million over 10 years, or $502 million over 15 years.

Only stable, predictable sources that can be locked in for years and transparently monitored suit a PFP: a trust fund, payments for ecosystem services, public-private partnership, blended finance, a donor contribution and green bonds. Together that is about $10.3 million a year, or $203 million over 15 years. Corporate donations matter but are too unpredictable.

05 Who and how

What it takes to launch

Strong political leadership

In a centralised system, a PFP's success depends on support at the highest level. The lead implementer could be the National Biodiversity Fund proposed by the President, or one of the national holdings.

NGO partner — ACBK

The Association for the Conservation of Biodiversity of Kazakhstan manages 423,000 ha of hunting land in the Kostanay region where hunting is banned — in effect, already an OECM.

Local communities

More than 800 hunting farms are an entry point for working with residents: jobs, tourism, wildlife management. Income from sustainable tourism could grow from $2.6 million in 2024 to $6.8 million by 2035.

The report also reviews governance risks based on the OECD's 2024 monitoring and proposes that the trust fund be managed by independent parties acceptable to donors. We urge policymakers and donors to support PFP design as a durable route to 30×30 and long-term conservation finance.
